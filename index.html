Live Pools under the Saturation Point

If k (the protocol parameter stakePoolTargetNum) goes from 500 to 1000, a stake pool saturates at about 38.9 million ADA. Above that point the rewards per ADA go down. The pools below are alive, make themselves known to the public, have less stake than that and are the only pool of their owner.

We are strongly in favor of decentralization. That is why we support raising k to 1000, and why this list starts from the saturation point at k = 1000. The pools that need more delegation the most are at the top: the list starts with the smallest pools.

How the list is made

Every day, from the Cardano chain, a pool is listed when it:

  • Has stake in the current epoch and is not retired.
  • Makes itself known to the public: it has a name, a ticker and a homepage (a web address) in its registered metadata.
  • Answers on at least one of its registered relays. A block is not asked: a pool that is waiting for stake cannot make one.
  • Meets the pledge it declared: its owners hold at least that much stake in the pool this epoch. A pool that does not meet its pledge earns no rewards in that epoch, and neither do its delegators.
  • Has less stake than the saturation point at k = 1000: the ADA outside the reserves, divided by 1000.
  • Is not run by an exchange or by one of the founding entities of Cardano (IOG, EMURGO, Cardano Foundation).
  • Is the only pool of its owner. An owner with two or more pools takes the place of another operator, so none of its pools is listed. When the owner is back to one pool, that pool can be listed again.

Pools of exchanges and of the founding entities are left out on purpose: this list is meant to spread stake over independent pool operators. The seven EMURGO pools that were handed over in September 2026 (now MURA1 to MURA7) stay off the list, because there is no transparency about the takeover. All of these are recognized by pool ID, so a pool that changes its name stays off the list.

Two pools are of one owner when they share an owner key or a reward account, when they belong to the same exchange or founding entity, or when two of these agree: the same name without its number, the same ticker without its digits, the same own website. These are the criteria of our Decentralization Monitor. The same relays or the same hosting do not count: they say who runs a pool, not whose it is.

A relay that does not answer from here is not proof that a pool is down: some pools only accept known peers. Still, a pool whose relays do not answer is not on this list: keeping its relays reachable is the responsibility of the pool operator.

A pool's name, ticker and homepage are its own registered metadata. They are read from the address the pool recorded on the chain, and used only when what is found there matches the hash the pool recorded with it. A pool without a homepage, or whose metadata cannot be read or no longer matches that hash, is not on this list: keeping its metadata in order is the responsibility of the pool operator too. A name is chosen by the pool itself. It is not proof of who is behind it.

Under the name of a pool, the list shows the governance roles the pool declares in its registered metadata, in the way the proposed CIP Disclosure of Governance Roles reads them: a DRep the pool says is its own, a seat on the Constitutional Committee, and whether the DRep declares the pool in return (reciprocal). The Cardano Constitution asks anyone who holds several governance roles to disclose this. Only a declaration counts. A shared name or website, a delegation or a vote is not a declaration and is not shown as one. No roles declared means that none is declared, not that there is no overlap. Metadata that cannot be read leaves the roles unknown.

The list is made by Smit Blockchain Operations, operator of pool BKIND, which is on it by the same rules as every other pool.

Who speaks for your ADA?

Who decides about Cardano? Until recently, mainly the companies that founded it. Since early 2025 that is the people who hold ADA: together they decide how the network changes and how the money in its treasury is spent. Cardano's constitution says the community governs itself, in a process that is open, transparent and protected from undue influence.

You take part through a representative, a DRep. Your DRep votes on your behalf, with the weight of your ADA. You choose who that is and you can switch at any time. Delegating does not move your ADA: you pay only the transaction fee.

Your voice only counts if your DRep votes the way you would. A DRep represents you, not themselves. So choose someone whose views you share, check how they vote and why, and switch when they no longer speak for you.

Why does it matter who holds the vote? Because power that sits with a few can be used by a few. A small group can be pressured, bought, or simply share the same interests, and then it decides for everyone: about the treasury, the rules, even the constitution itself. Spread over many independent voices, that power is hard to capture. Decentralization is not only about who runs the network, but also about who decides over it.

Today very few hold that power.

  • Of 37.5 billion ADA in circulation, 5.2 billion (13.9%) takes part in the vote.
  • 21.6 billion ADA (57.6%) has no DRep at all. 10.5 billion (28.0%) is set to Always Abstain and never votes.
  • 547 DReps are inactive: they have not voted for at least 100 days. The 186 million ADA delegated to them counts for nothing.
  • Of the ADA that votes, 5 DReps together can block any treasury withdrawal or protocol parameter change. 3 can block a change to the constitution. 20 together carry the DRep vote on a treasury withdrawal.
  • In the last six months only 39.6% of DRep votes came with a rationale anyone can read (a public explanation of the vote, whose link works and whose content matches the hash on the chain). 139 active DReps, holding 23.9% of the voting power, did not explain a single vote.

Choose a DRep who votes in line with your views and explains why. If several DReps fit, a smaller one helps spread the vote. The list starts with the DReps that voted and explained their vote most often; among equals, the smallest comes first.

BKIND is in this list on the same terms as every other DRep. BKIND is both a stake pool operator and a DRep.

How the list is made

Every day, from the Cardano chain:

  • Every DRep that the Cardano node reports as registered and active is listed, except the DReps of the founding entities of Cardano (IOG, EMURGO, Cardano Foundation).
  • Voting power is the ADA delegated to the DRep in the current epoch, and its share of all ADA that votes.
  • Voted counts the governance actions open for voting in the last six months that the DRep voted on, out of all it could vote on: for a DRep that registered later, only the actions submitted since it registered, plus any older action it did vote on.
  • Explained counts the actions on which the DRep voted with a rationale anyone can read, out of all it could vote on: the link works, the content matches the hash on the chain, and it holds text. A dead link, a wrong hash or an empty document does not count. The list is ordered by this share, then by the share voted, then from small to large.
  • The name is the DRep's own registered metadata, shown only when it matches the hash recorded on the chain; otherwise the DRep shows with its ID.
  • A name is chosen by the DRep itself. It is not proof of who is behind it: the DRep ID is what you delegate to.
  • Under the name, the list shows the governance roles the DRep declares in its registered metadata, in the way the proposed CIP Disclosure of Governance Roles reads them: a stake pool the DRep says is its own, a seat on the Constitutional Committee, and whether the pool declares the DRep in return (reciprocal). The Cardano Constitution asks anyone who holds several governance roles to disclose this. Only a declaration counts. A shared name or website, a delegation or a vote is not a declaration and is not shown as one. No roles declared means that none is declared, not that there is no overlap. Metadata that cannot be read leaves the roles unknown.

DReps of the founding entities are left out on purpose. Cardano's own roadmap describes the goal of its governance as a network that is “no longer under IOHK's management”, with its future “in the hands of the community”. This list follows that direction: it is meant to spread the vote over independent voices. The founding entities are free to vote as DReps, and their voting power still counts in the figures above. They are recognized by DRep ID, so a DRep that changes its name stays off the list.

About this dashboard and how it is made

Cardano is only as decentralized as the power over it is spread. This page shows in two views how the vote and the stake are spread today, measured from the chain, and where your own ADA sits.

  • Every day, from the Cardano chain, by the same job as the pool list and the DRep list. The voting power and the DReps who together can block or carry are the same figures as there.
  • Owners are grouped as in our Decentralization Monitor: Pools that share an owner key or a reward account, or belong to the same exchange or founding entity, are of one owner. So are pools that agree on two of these three: Their name, their ticker family and their own website. The figures count the fewest owners who together hold more than a third of all stake, and half of all stake. A third is the measure most trackers use to compare blockchains. Half is the threshold that counts for Cardano, whose consensus rests on an honest majority of the stake.
  • A DRep can block together with others when their voting power, with Always No Confidence on the same side, is more than a third of the ADA that votes. They carry a treasury withdrawal when they reach the threshold of 67% on their own.
  • A DRep is marked as also running a stake pool when our Decentralization Monitor links it to a pool, by point 5 of its method. That link starts from the chain: A key of the pool delegates its vote to the DRep, confirmed by the chain or by what the DRep and the pool publish. All pools of the same owner count, with their stake together, as in the monitor. So this site counts one set of such DReps everywhere. A DRep is marked for the Constitutional Committee when it says in its own metadata that it is a member of a body with a seat on the committee, or by Intersect's own anchor on the chain. When both apply, the color is that of the committee and both reasons are shown. Point at a bar to see the reason.
  • Every other DRep is shown as DRep only. That means no other role was found in what it declares, not that there is none. A DRep that calls itself a stake pool operator but is not linked to a pool stays DRep only, and the reason says so.
  • The counts under each list say how many there are and how many of them are in the chart. A DRep is registered by the state of the node today, and active when its voting power counts this epoch. A pool owner is counted when its pools hold stake.
  • The lookup shows the latest delegation on the chain. A change counts from the snapshot of the next epoch.
  • In the stake pools view an owner is orange when our Decentralization Monitor links one of its pools to a DRep, dark grey when it has several pools and light grey when it has one. The chart shows every owner with a pool that made a block in the past year, the pools our monitor counts as active. An exchange or a founding entity carries the name of that organization. Any other owner with several pools carries the name its pools share, without their numbering. An owner with one pool carries its ticker. A click on a bar opens the largest pool of that owner on Cardanoscan. The line under the chart says what the other owners hold together.

The dashboard is made by Smit Blockchain Operations, operator of pool BKIND and a DRep, which is in these figures on the same terms as everyone else.

We take part in on-chain governance in two capacities, as the operator of stake pool BKIND (pool1m83drqwlugdt9jn7jkz8hx3pne53acfkd539d9cj8yr92dr4k9y) and as a registered DRep (drep1ytc6867ae0xmkekvmex79r9akyy28eu8nu03jf3xu9fle6c82l4eq). Both are operated by the same person, as Article II, Section 3 of the Cardano Constitution asks us to disclose, and we publish the reasoning behind every vote we cast.

Security runs through everything we do. We build and open-source air-gapped tools for stake pool operators, DReps and wallet holders, so the safety of your keys never depends on software you are forced to trust blindly.

We also audit the Cardano ledger directly, with our own tooling written in Haskell. With it we do hands-on on-chain forensics: we trace how value really moves and check claims against what the ledger itself records, so a genuine problem can be told apart from a false alarm.

One idea ties it together: decentralize and harmonize. Blockchain is at its best when it helps people rely on one another instead of on a handful of gatekeepers.
